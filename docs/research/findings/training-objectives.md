# Training Objectives & Learning Signals

## Summary

Nearly all published MTG draft-pick models are trained as imitation learning / behavior cloning: predict the human's actual pick. The field has progressed in HOW it imitates rather than WHAT it imitates. The earliest large-scale work (Ward et al. 2020, Draftsim) framed drafting as multi-class classification with softmax cross-entropy over the card vocabulary, masked to the current pack, reaching ~48.7% top-1 accuracy. Bertram, Fuernkranz and Mueller reframed it as contextual preference ranking with a Siamese network and triplet loss (pool=anchor, picked card=positive, unpicked cards=negatives), embedding decks and cards in a shared space; this jumped accuracy to ~54-64% and yielded interpretable embeddings. Their 2024 follow-up adapts CLIP's InfoNCE loss (a masked, row-wise contrastive cross-entropy) to beat triplet loss while removing triplet-mining headaches. Crucially, pure behavior cloning is capped by human-data quality and bakes in popularity/conformity bias, so it produces human-LIKE rather than GOOD picks. Two distinct signals point beyond cloning: (1) win-rate-weighted imitation and reward modeling using 17lands aggregate metrics (GIH WR, IWD, ALSA), and (2) closing the loop with simulated games / self-play (Saxe), which is the only route to picks that provably exceed the demonstrators. The strongest practical recipe is contextual-ranking imitation as a base, filtered to high-win-rate players and reweighted/regularized by win-rate signals to debias conformity, with 17lands metrics used as auxiliary regression targets and a learned reward/value head for eventual offline-RL refinement.

## Approaches

### Imitation learning via masked multi-class classification (softmax cross-entropy)  
*Maturity: production-proven*

Treat each pick as a classification problem: an MLP takes the current collection (and optionally the pack) and outputs a probability over the whole card set; the output is element-wise multiplied by a pack indicator mask so only available cards are scorable, then trained with cross-entropy against the card the human actually took. This is the Draftsim NNetBot (Ward et al. 2020) and the statisticaldrafting.com MLP.

**How it applies:** Directly predicts the human's actual pick. Generalization to unseen cards is the weak point: the output layer is a fixed card-vocabulary head, so a new set requires retraining and a new head. Generalization improves only if cards are represented by features (cost, color, type, oracle text, 17lands metrics) rather than one-hot IDs - the 'generalised card representations' direction noted as future work by Bertram et al.

**Tradeoffs:** Simple, fast, stable; strong baseline (~48.7% top-1, Ward et al.; ~70% agreement with top players on statisticaldrafting.com when filtered to strong players). But: evaluates each card largely in isolation, fixed-vocabulary head blocks cross-set transfer, and it inherits all popularity/conformity bias in the data. Caps at human quality.

### Contextual preference ranking with Siamese networks + triplet loss  
*Maturity: research-validated*

Bertram, Fuernkranz and Mueller (2021). Embed the pool (anchor a), the picked card (positive p), and each unpicked card (negative n) with a shared network; train with triplet loss max(d(a,p)-d(a,n)+m, 0) so the picked card sits closer to the pool embedding. At inference, score every card in the pack by distance to the pool embedding - no pairwise comparisons needed because embedding distances are transitive. Distance to the empty set yields a context-free card power ranking.

**How it applies:** Models picks as preferences-in-context, explicitly capturing synergy ('cards that go together') rather than standalone value, which is the heart of good drafting. Beats the classification baseline (53.69% even at embedding dim D=2; up to ~64% at higher D; >56% relative accuracy gain, >83% reduction in pick distance vs NNetBot). The embedding space generalizes better conceptually because cards live in a learned metric space, but card inputs are still one-hot, so unseen cards need representation features to transfer.

**Tradeoffs:** Captures synergy/context and produces interpretable, smoother card-strength rankings. Costs: requires triplet mining (sampling and mining strategy materially affects accuracy - they found random mining beat hard mining); three forward passes per update; positive examples skew toward strong cards so weak-card weakness is under-learned. Still imitation, so still conformity-biased.

### Contextual InfoNCE / CLIP-adapted contrastive loss  
*Maturity: research-validated*

Bertram et al. (2024). Adapt CLIP's InfoNCE to contextual preference ranking: build a cosine-similarity matrix between pool and card encoders, but MASK out comparisons that are invalid in context (cards not in the same pack) and apply cross-entropy only row-wise (since a pool can have multiple valid positives across samples and column-wise comparisons are context-agnostic). Uses separate card and pool encoders rather than a fully weight-shared Siamese tower.

**How it applies:** Same imitation target (predict human pick) but a better-conditioned contrastive objective: uses all in-pack negatives at once instead of one mined triplet, avoiding the skewed positive/negative frequency of triplet sampling. Outperforms both triplet loss and vanilla CLIP on top-1 accuracy with ~10% training-time overhead. Best-in-class among the imitation objectives for this dataset.

**Tradeoffs:** Better accuracy and no triplet mining, but vanilla InfoNCE fails out-of-the-box here (out-of-context comparisons), so the masking adaptation is essential. The authors explicitly flag that InfoNCE still does not natively encode the context of a preference into the loss - an open problem. Still pure imitation.

### Win-rate-weighted / win-rate-filtered imitation  
*Maturity: production-proven*

Instead of weighting every human pick equally, filter or reweight training examples by outcome quality. statisticaldrafting.com filters the 17lands dataset to high-volume, high-win-rate players to 'describe the top player metagame'; a stronger variant weights each pick/draft by the resulting deck's match win rate (importance/advantage weighting), which is the offline-RL idea of advantage-weighted behavior cloning applied to drafts.

**How it applies:** Moves the target from 'what the average human did' toward 'what good outcomes looked like'. Filtering to strong players already lifts agreement-with-good-picks to ~70%. Outcome weighting is the cleanest way to escape conformity bias while staying in stable supervised training - it upweights picks that led to winning decks and downweights crowd-following picks that lost.

**Tradeoffs:** Cheap and stable; directly targets GOOD picks. But the win signal is deck-level and noisy/confounded (a pick's credit is entangled with the whole deck and player skill), and aggressive filtering shrinks data. Needs care to avoid survivorship bias (winning decks over-represent already-strong archetypes).

### Reward modeling from match outcomes + offline RL / self-play (closing the loop)  
*Maturity: experimental*

Learn a value/reward model that maps a pool or deck to expected match win rate, then optimize picks against it (advantage-weighted regression, Q-learning over the factored card-action space, or PPO/self-play once a game simulator exists). Saxe's project reports reaching Mythic at ~66% win rate over 23 drafts on Arena; Ward et al. and Saxe both note that testing pool strength in simulated games is what 'closes the loop' to enable RL.

**How it applies:** This is the only family that can exceed the human demonstrators, because the learning signal is the actual objective (winning) rather than mimicry. The pick policy is rewarded for the eventual deck's performance, so it can discover non-consensus-but-strong picks that imitation would never surface.

**Tradeoffs:** Hardest to build: needs a deckbuild model AND a game-playing agent/simulator for reward; sparse, high-variance, long-horizon credit assignment (45 picks -> one deck -> many games); offline RL over a huge factored action space is unstable and can exploit reward-model errors. Largely experimental for MTG; full self-play for real MTG games does not yet exist per Bertram et al. 2024.

### 17lands aggregate metrics as features / labels / auxiliary supervision  
*Maturity: production-proven*

Use 17lands per-card aggregates three ways. As FEATURES: feed GIH WR, GP WR, ALSA, ATA, IWD into the card encoder (this also enables cross-set generalization since a brand-new card has these stats after a few days of data). As LABELS: regress the model's card score onto GIH WR / adjusted win rate, turning the model into a card-quality predictor. As AUXILIARY SUPERVISION: add a multi-task head predicting GIH WR / ALSA alongside the imitation head to regularize the representation toward quality and openness.

**How it applies:** GIH WR (win rate when in hand, drawn or not, even if uncast) is 17lands' least-biased card-quality stat and the best single quality label. ALSA (average last seen at) encodes scarcity/wheelability - useful for 'staying open' and for expected-availability reasoning. IWD (improvement when drawn = win rate when the card was in hand minus win rate when it was not) is a within-deck quality proxy. Using these as features is the most direct lever for generalizing to UNSEEN cards, since the model can score a never-seen card from its metrics.

**Tradeoffs:** GIH WR is confounded: biased toward controlling decks (longer games see more cards) and toward strong archetypes/players, so card credit is entangled with deck quality - mtgds proposes a regression-based 'adjusted win rate' controlling for deck composition, pairwise synergy, on-the-play, mulligans and player win rate. GP WR is ~half noise (games where the card was never drawn). IWD is acknowledged as flawed. Using raw aggregates as labels can re-import metagame/popularity bias and ignores context/synergy (these are marginal, not contextual, stats).

### Conformity / popularity-bias mitigation for imitation  
*Maturity: research-validated*

Imitation reproduces popularity bias: cards the crowd takes get reinforced regardless of strength (analogous to popularity/conformity bias in recommender systems, where observed behavior mixes true quality with a conformity effect of following community norms). Mitigations transferable to drafting: (1) outcome/win-rate reweighting or filtering to strong players; (2) inverse-popularity reweighting / regularizers that penalize scoring by raw pick-frequency; (3) propensity/IPS-style debiasing of the imitation loss; (4) supervising with confounder-adjusted card quality (adjusted win rate) instead of raw picks; (5) re-ranking to add archetype diversity / staying-open priors (Saxe's explicit 'staying open decays over time' prior).

**How it applies:** Directly serves the GOOD-not-just-human goal. Ward et al. observed pure imitation bots mis-learn 'raredrafting' (humans greedily taking rares) - a textbook conformity artifact a Bayesian count model reproduced and the NN partly suppressed. Saxe explicitly avoids rote cloning (his bot hits 91% on a P3P1 where humans are only ~80% correct), showing principled priors + debiasing can beat the training distribution.

**Tradeoffs:** Win-rate reweighting needs reliable outcome data and adds confounding of its own (deck/player skill); propensity/IPS debiasing is high-variance; diversity re-ranking trades raw imitation accuracy for openness/robustness. Adjusted-win-rate labels need a fitted causal/regression model that itself can be misspecified.

## Recommendations

- Use a contextual-ranking imitation objective as the base, not flat classification. The InfoNCE/CLIP-masked loss (Bertram 2024) is the current best for this exact task and avoids triplet mining; triplet loss (Bertram 2021) is the proven fallback. Both model synergy/context, which is what separates good picks from standalone card grades.
- Do NOT stop at vanilla behavior cloning - it provably caps at human quality and bakes in conformity bias. Add at least one debiasing/quality signal: (a) filter or reweight training examples by the resulting deck's match win rate (advantage-weighted BC), and/or (b) filter to high-win-rate players as statisticaldrafting.com does.
- Represent cards by FEATURES, not one-hot IDs, to generalize to unseen cards. Concatenate static attributes (cost, color, type, rarity, parsed oracle-text embeddings) WITH 17lands aggregates (GIH WR, ALSA, ATA, IWD). A brand-new card then gets a sensible score from its metrics, and you can retrain across sets without swapping output heads.
- Prefer GIH WR as your card-quality label/feature, but adjust for confounders. Raw GIH WR favors controlling decks and strong archetypes; fit an mtgds-style adjusted win rate (regress out deck composition, synergy, on-the-play, mulligans, player skill) before using it as a label, or use it only as an auxiliary multi-task target so it regularizes rather than dominates.
- Add an auxiliary head that predicts ALSA. This teaches expected availability/wheelability and directly supports 'staying open' reasoning, complementing the pick-imitation head without contaminating it.
- To get picks that EXCEED humans, plan a two-stage path: (1) imitation/ranking + win-rate weighting for a strong, stable base policy; (2) close the loop with a deckbuilder and a game simulator to learn a win-rate reward/value model, then fine-tune with offline RL (advantage-weighted regression first, it is the most stable) or self-play. Saxe's Mythic/66% result shows the loop-closing path works.
- Explicitly mitigate conformity bias: penalize scoring by raw pick-frequency, consider inverse-propensity reweighting of the imitation loss, and add diversity/openness re-ranking. Validate against win-rate-based metrics (does the bot's deck win?) not just top-1 agreement with humans - high human-agreement can mean you faithfully reproduced a bad crowd consensus.

## Open questions

- How to inject the CONTEXT of a preference directly into the contrastive loss - Bertram et al. (2024) explicitly flag that InfoNCE still compares somewhat context-agnostically and call this the main open problem.
- Credit assignment from a 45-pick draft -> one built deck -> many noisy games: which offline-RL formulation (advantage-weighted BC, factored-action Q-learning, decision transformer) best handles the long horizon and huge factored action space without exploiting reward-model error?
- Can a confounder-adjusted win rate (mtgds-style) be learned jointly end-to-end with the pick policy so card-quality labels and pick prediction co-train, rather than fitting adjusted WR as a separate preprocessing step?
- How to disentangle true card quality from the conformity effect in observed picks with a principled causal/IPS estimator for drafting, where propensities (what the crowd takes) are themselves learned and uncertain.
- Cross-set zero-shot generalization: how well do feature-based card representations (oracle-text embeddings + early-window 17lands stats) predict good picks for a freshly released set before enough 17lands data accumulates?
- Is a full MTG game simulator/self-play loop feasible at the fidelity needed for a trustworthy win-rate reward, given that no strong full-game MTG playing agent currently exists (per Bertram et al. 2024)?
- How to weight match-outcome reward against imitation so the model stays in realistic, legal, human-sensible pick distributions while still discovering non-consensus strong picks (avoiding reward hacking and unstable archetype commitments).

## Sources

- [AI solutions for drafting in Magic: the Gathering (Ward, Mills, Brooks, Khakhalin, Troha; IEEE CoG 2021)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.00655) *(paper)* — Frames drafting as masked multi-class classification; NNetBot (deep NN, cross-entropy, 20 epochs, pack-masked argmax) gets 48.67% top-1 vs 44.54% expert heuristic and 22% random over 107,949 human drafts. Notes imitation goal is human-likeness not objectively-best picks, and that imitation bots mis-handle 'raredrafting' (a conformity artifact). Releases the first large public draft dataset.
- [Predicting Human Card Selection in MTG with Contextual Preference Ranking (Bertram, Fuernkranz, Mueller; IEEE CoG 2021)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.11864) *(paper)* — Siamese network + triplet loss with pool=anchor, picked=positive, unpicked=negative; distance-to-empty-set gives a card power ranking. Beats classification baseline (53.69% even at D=2, up to ~64%; >56% relative accuracy gain, >83% lower pick distance). Captures synergy/context; positive examples skew toward strong cards so weak-card weakness is under-learned.
- [Contrastive Learning of Preferences with a Contextual InfoNCE Loss (Bertram, Fuernkranz, Mueller; 2024)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.05898) *(paper)* — Adapts CLIP/InfoNCE to contextual preference ranking via a masked, row-wise cross-entropy over in-pack cards; outperforms triplet loss and vanilla CLIP while removing triplet mining (~10% overhead). Uses 17lands human-selection data. States no strong full-game self-play MTG agent exists yet, and that encoding context INTO the loss remains open.
- [Bot Drafting the Hard Way: A New Drafting Model (Ryan Saxe, Draftsim)](https://draftsim.com/ryan-saxe-bot-model/) *(blog)* — Rejects rote behavior cloning; uses mathematical priors (per-archetype linear models via K-means clustering, archetypal bias weighting, 'staying open decays over time'). Beats the data: ~91% on a P3P1 where humans are ~80% correct - a concrete example of producing better-than-human, debiased picks.
- [RyanSaxe/mtg: State of the Art MTG Draft and DeckBuilder AI (GitHub)](https://github.com/RyanSaxe/mtg) *(repo)* — Transformer-based draft AI plus a deckbuilder; reports reaching Mythic rank at ~66% win rate over 23 drafts, claimed first AI to do so. Illustrates the self-play/closing-the-loop direction (need a deckbuilder + game agent to turn picks into win-rate reward). README does not disclose the exact loss; train-it-yourself.
- [Statistical Drafting - About (statisticaldrafting.com)](https://statisticaldrafting.com/about) *(blog)* — Small PyTorch MLP (BatchNorm, dropout, decaying LR) takes collection+pack vectors, outputs ratings, pack used as a mask to zero out unavailable cards. Trained on 17lands data FILTERED to high-volume high-win-rate players (~70% agreement with top players) - a concrete win-rate-filtered imitation pipeline to reduce conformity bias.
- [Using Win Rate Data (17Lands Blog)](https://blog.17lands.com/posts/using-win-rate-data/) *(blog)* — Defines GP WR (about half noise from games where the card wasn't drawn), OH WR, GD WR, GIH WR (drawn at any point, least biased, no need to cast). Caveats: GIH WR biases toward controlling decks (longer games) and confounds deck/archetype quality; treat WR as a starting point, strongest signal early in a draft.
- [Knowledge and Power: Estimating Adjusted Win Rate in MTG Limited (mtgds)](https://mtgds.wordpress.com/2022/02/28/knowledge-and-power-estimating-adjusted-win-rate-in-magic-the-gathering-limited/) *(blog)* — Proposes Adjusted Win Rate via linear regression controlling for other cards in the deck, pairwise synergy, on-the-play, mulligans and player win rate, isolating each card's marginal contribution. Documents co-occurrence/deck-quality confounding in GP WR and game-length bias in GIH WR - the case for confounder-adjusted labels over raw 17lands aggregates.

## Source verification

**Overall confidence:** high. All 8 cited sources are real and reachable. Five (Saxe blog, Saxe GitHub, statisticaldrafting, 17lands blog, mtgds) were fully fetched and their attributed claims confirmed nearly verbatim. The three arXiv sources returned unparseable PDF binaries from the fetcher (a tool limitation, not a sign of fabrication); I re-verified each via its arXiv abstract page, which confirmed exact titles, authors, venues, and core method/framing for all three. The fine-grained numeric claims inside those papers could not be re-confirmed from abstracts alone, so I treat them as plausible-but-not-independently-verified rather than wrong. No source appears hallucinated or misattributed. The main soft spots are (a) precise in-paper numbers I couldn't re-read, and (b) two interpretive overreaches in the synthesis: framing Saxe's archetype-fit metric as general pick correctness, and citing Saxe's repo (an imitation/transformer model with an anecdotal Mythic run) as evidence for the self-play/reward-modeling/offline-RL approach family, which it does not actually implement. The 17lands metric definitions, adjusted-win-rate methodology, and conformity/confounding discussions are all accurately grounded in the cited sources.

**Flagged claims:**
- ⚠️ The '107,949 human drafts' figure for Ward et al. is more precise than what the abstract states ('over 100,000'); the exact count could not be re-confirmed from the accessible abstract page (the full-PDF body was unparseable by the fetcher). Likely correct but flagged as not independently re-verified.
- ⚠️ The detailed accuracy figures attributed to Bertram et al. 2021 (53.69% at D=2, up to ~64%, >56% relative accuracy gain, >83% reduction in pick distance) and Ward et al. (48.67% vs 44.54% expert vs 22% random) could not be re-confirmed because the arXiv PDF bodies were not machine-readable by the fetcher; only the abstracts/method framing were verifiable. They are plausible and consistent but should be treated as not independently re-confirmed in this check.
- ⚠️ Bertram et al. 2024 abstract does not explicitly mention MTG or 17lands (it says 'collectable card games'); the research's specific claims that it 'uses 17lands human-selection data', has '~10% overhead', and 'states no strong full-game self-play MTG agent exists yet' are not confirmable from the abstract and rely on the paper body, which could not be parsed.
- ⚠️ Saxe's '91% vs ~80%' result is specifically about taking cards in the PROPER ARCHETYPE at P3P1, not general pick correctness. The research JSON repeatedly frames this as 'humans are ~80% correct' and 'better-than-human, debiased picks', which slightly overstates an archetype-fit metric as overall pick quality.
- ⚠️ Saxe's GitHub 'Mythic at 66% over 23 drafts' is a single anecdotal self-report by the author with no independent verification; the research presents it as evidence the loop-closing/self-play path 'works'. The claim is accurately quoted but the underlying evidence is weak/anecdotal, and the repo README does not actually demonstrate self-play or a win-rate reward model — it is a transformer imitation/deckbuild AI, so citing it as evidence for the 'reward modeling + offline RL / self-play' approach family overstates what the source shows.
